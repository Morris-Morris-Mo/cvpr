**Figure 1. From late supplementation to geometry-disagreement-aware ranking.** Independent vehicle and cooperative paths produce different final predictions. Same-class association identifies matched and single-branch candidates. GeoRank combines cross-branch geometric relationships with original scores to assess quality. Blue and orange denote the two prediction branches, and teal denotes our output processing. Box positions are conceptual illustrations, not actual samples or measured ranking results.

**Figure 2. GeoRank pipeline and controlled interventions.** A shared front end generates candidates and parent relationships. Path A introduces explicit disagreement before deduplication and reruns the complete output pipeline and basic tracking. Path B fixes candidates and box geometry after base deduplication, applying explicit disagreement penalties or learned GeoRank to update detection scores only. The fixed-candidate explicit intervention directly tests geometry's independent contribution, while the learned path tests joint relationship scoring. GT is used only for learned fitting labels.

**Figure 3. Additional gains from geometric disagreement ranking.** (a–b) On all ten scenes and 1,490 frames, explicit linear/multiplicative penalties improve base AP from 39.26 to **39.86 / 39.87**, with actual AMOTA of 44.61 / 44.68. (c) On the five-scene internal-validation holdout, learned GeoRank improves base AP from 38.70 to **40.12 (+1.42)**, with fixed candidates and box geometry and without rerunning tracking. Each panel explicitly identifies its scope; comparisons are within groups only.

**Figure 4. Ranking effects of geometric relationship scoring.** Left: AP at different GT matching tolerances over five held-out scenes and 745 frames. Right: representative feature configurations on the same candidate set; all eight appear in Table 3. The 0.5 m and 1 m criteria are stricter localization-matching tolerances, with gains of **2.38 and 2.21 points**. Complete features differ from score relationships plus a matched indicator in both geometry and single-branch source indicators. The controlled intervention in Table 5(a) tests geometry's independent contribution.

**Figure 5. Practical effects of late supplementation.** Statistics use a fixed operating point of vehicle scores at least 0.20 and center-matching distance below 2 m. Base fusion retains 505 of 594 vehicle-only GT observations. Relative to cooperative outputs, TP increases by 446 and FP by 51. Relative to direct merging and deduplication, TP decreases by 114 and FP by 1,190. These statistics explain supplementation and filtering trade-offs; fixed-threshold TP/FP does not replace AP.

**Figure S1. Base-fusion parameter sensitivity.** Four one-factor sweeps cover 16 unique configurations. Defaults are marked separately, and vehicle AP and AMOTA are plotted separately. All curves use complete 55 m validation results; axis-wise maxima do not replace the main result.

**Figure S2. A successful late-supplementation case.** In the same 55 m sample, base fusion retains a target detected by the independent vehicle branch but missed by the cooperative branch. Existing predictions and GT are visualized. This illustrates candidate supplementation, not learned rescoring correcting box positions.

**Figure S3. A geometric-fusion failure case.** The cooperative parent has center error approximately 1.821 m, which becomes approximately 2.013 m after fusion, crossing the 2 m matching tolerance, despite a fused score of approximately 0.969. Neither high scores nor geometric fusion guarantee correct matching, motivating separate analysis of geometry processing and quality ranking.

**Figure S4. Localization-quality relationships between paired parents.** Only actual paired vehicle parents that individually match the same GT are counted. The vehicle parent has smaller center error in 50.30% of cases. This conditional-subset analysis does not estimate full-dataset accuracy or causal gains.
